*an arche* takes its title from the Greek ἀν-αρχή — the negation of *arche*. Where
*arche* names a first principle, a governing origin, a ruling force that determines
and organises what follows from it, *an-arche* names the condition prior to and
beneath any such principle: a state without ruler, without founding ground, without
the kind of origin that commands. This is not chaos. It is the more unsettling
condition of a ground that is itself groundless — what Reiner Schürmann, reading
Heidegger, called ontological anarchy: the factor that simultaneously permits the
emergence of any determination while refusing to be fixed as the stable foundation
of that determination. An-arche is not the absence of order but the impossibility of
locating the origin of order in any single, sovereign principle.

The piece inhabits this impossibility. It does not organise itself around pitch,
harmony, or any of the governing principles through which Western instrumental music
conventionally establishes its *arche*. Instead it works at the layer beneath those
principles — the layer of raw physical contact between bow, string and body — where
no single force is sovereign and where every apparent origin is already distributed,
already multiple, already unstable. Bow pressure, string tension, contact point and
the resistance of matter against the moving arm are not means toward musical
meaning; they are the an-archic ground from which this piece refuses to depart.

The work is scored for one cello shared by three performers and quadraphonic sound
diffusion. The three performers do not divide the instrument efficiently or
practically. They embody three distinct modes of relation to its an-archic ground —
three figures of agency operating without a common governing principle, each
approaching the instrument from a different phenomenological position, none of them
sovereign over the whole.

## The First Performer — immediate corporeal force

A seated cellist who holds one bow in each hand simultaneously: right hand in the
lower position near the bridge, left hand in the upper position near the
fingerboard. This is the most immediate, least mediated presence in the work — the
animal, bodily axis of the piece. The task is not to realise pitches or navigate
harmonic structures but to inhabit a continuous field of pressure: to sustain,
transform and destabilise friction between bow and string across the full length of
the instrument. The double-bow setup allows two independent contact points and two
independent pressure trajectories to coexist, often in contrary motion, contrary
rotation or contrary pressure state.

The vocabulary is drawn entirely from the domain of physical force: bidirectional
overpressure, contact-point glissando in contrary motion, sul ponticello tremolo
against sul tasto overpressure, scratch tone alternating with silence, flautando at
threshold pressure, dead bow against accelerando détaché, col legno battuto and
tratto simultaneously, ricochet in rhythmic canon, bilateral circular bowing in
contrary rotation. This performer does not "play" the cello in the conventional
sense. They struggle with it, press against it, and are pressed back.

## The Second Performer — structural intervention and scordatura

This performer operates at the level of condition rather than immediate gesture.
Where the first is submerged in the physical present of friction, the second acts
consciously on the conditions under which that friction produces sound, controlling
scordatura in real time — retuning individual strings by specified amounts over
specified time spans while the first performer continues bowing. These interventions
do not interrupt the surface of the piece; they alter what the bows encounter
without announcing themselves as events.

At selected moments this performer enters the first performer's activity — placing a
hand on strings, neck or tailpiece to produce chords, double stops and resonance
configurations that the first performer, both hands occupied with bows, cannot
independently realise. At others they play the body of the instrument rhythmically,
imposing pulse, interference or rhythmic counterpoint on the continuous pressure
field. The second performer is the reflective layer — the figure who surveys the
situation from a slight distance and intervenes in its structural conditions with
deliberate intent, without ever assuming full control.

## The Third Performer — material transformation and preparation

Seated directly in front of the cello, facing the instrument, between it and the
audience. This position is not incidental: this performer is inside the performance
rather than adjacent to it, working on the instrument's material surface as a
continuous co-author of its identity.

Their function is to introduce, manipulate and remove some ten to fifteen physical
preparations across the duration of the piece — an alligator clip on the afterlength,
a glass slide against a string, a sponge block behind the bridge, a metal chain
across the tailpiece, a strip of foil woven through the strings, a rubber superball
held against the lower plate. These are not applied before the performance begins.
They are applied, moved, altered and removed during the piece, so that preparation is
not setup but action — musical time. What begins as an object placed on the
instrument gradually becomes a playable element as the performer discovers how to
activate, reposition and sound it. Preparations become instruments; the boundary
between preparation and performance dissolves.

## Spatial framework

The quadraphonic system extends the an-archic distribution of force into the room.
Analysis of the first performer's bowing — pressure density, spectral centroid,
contact point, noise-to-tone ratio — drives diffusion and transformation in the
loudspeaker space, so that the physical forces inscribed in the score become spatial
events surrounding the audience. The acoustic cello and the electronic field are not
separate layers; the electronic layer is the interior life of the acoustic surface
made audible across the room.

The speaker layout — conventional square, immersive ring, or diagonal diamond — is
chosen in consultation with the acoustics of the specific venue, with the consistent
goal that no stable front, no sovereign source of sound and no fixed origin can be
located. In the immersive ring configuration there is no stage: performers work at
floor level, inside a single sector of the ring, in the same continuous space as the
audience. The room, like the instrument, has no single arche.

## Live machine listening — FluCoMa

The electroacoustic layer is realised in real time through FluCoMa (Fluid Corpus
Manipulation) running in Max/MSP. The live cello signal is continuously analysed
across loudness, spectral centroid, mel-frequency cepstral coefficients, onset
detection and the noise-to-tonality ratio produced by the first performer's pressure
states. Five microphone zones feed it — bridge, fingerboard, lower bout, afterlength
and a room channel — so that the double-bow dynamic can be analysed as independent
streams with distinct timbral identities. The extracted features drive quadraphonic
diffusion, corpus-based playback and transformation, and parameter mappings for
filtering, convolution and spectral processing. The same analysis is routed as MIDI
to a DMX system, so the light in the room is governed by the same pressure states as
the sound.

The machine does not interpret the performance; it archives it continuously, matches
incoming material against its own accumulating record, and responds from within that
archive — a listener that acts from memory rather than intention.

This mechanism is not incidental to the conceptual framework. The FluCoMa system
constitutes its own *arche* within the work: an archival, governing intelligence that
organises the electronic layer according to principles derived entirely from the
acoustic surface it observes. Yet this arche, like the others, is not sovereign. It
does not compose, decide or control. It responds to conditions it did not set, using
a corpus it did not choose, in a space shaped by performers whose actions it cannot
predict. It is a principle of order that is itself ordered by something prior to it —
the pressure of a bow, the resistance of a string, the decision of a hand. The
machine listener becomes one more node in the plural, non-hierarchical field the
piece proposes: a fourth agency without authority, an intelligence that archives but
does not govern.

## Notation

The first performer's notation is written in proportional time with separate staves
for each bow, using line thickness for pressure density, vertical position for
contact region, and graphic symbols for gesture type and direction. The third
performer reads a topographical clef: a cello diagram at the head of the system
functions as a spatial rather than pitched staff, combined with pictograms for object
type, location and timing, and a secondary staff for gestures made through those
objects.

## Before the first sound

Two bundles of Palo Santo are lit at two points in front of the performance zone and
allowed to smoulder, releasing smoke slowly into the room throughout. The wood has
been used for millennia across the Andes as a cleansing material, understood not
symbolically but as an active agent that changes the conditions of a room and the
people in it before any action has begun. Its perceptual effect is lateral rather
than focused: the listener becomes more present, more permeable to subtle input, less
defended against the ambiguous — which is the condition the piece requires.
