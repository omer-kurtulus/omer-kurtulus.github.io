The contemporary composer of art music operates within an unprecedented ontological
condition: an over-saturated archive of organised sound. What would once have
required years of travel, institutional access and material resources is now
reachable in minutes. Historic recordings of gagaku, early studio works from GRM and
WDR, mid-century electroacoustic pieces, spectral and microtonal repertoires,
extended-technique practices and recent mixed-media works coexist within the same
searchable present.

This is not a quantitative expansion of available material but a qualitative rupture
in how time, geography and musical practice relate to one another. Music is no
longer experienced as a chronologically ordered sequence of periods. The composer
encounters a dense simultaneity of compressed strata — a shakuhachi solo on shellac,
Lachenmann's *musique concrète instrumentale*, Grisey's *Partiels*, a recent Rebecca
Saunders piece and a noise improvisation circulating side by side. The contemporary
is not a thin slice of the present but a thick, crowded time.

The nineteenth-century model imagined the composer as a figure of refinement,
selecting and sublimating a limited set of materials into *Kunstmusik*. That model
depended on scarcity. The present condition is almost its inverse: not scarcity but
excessive saturation, in which virtually everything has already been recorded,
notated, analysed and recirculated. The central question shifts from *what can I
compose* to *what does it mean to compose when so much has already been composed,
archived and technically manipulable*.

**Sonic archaeology** is this article's answer — not archaeoacoustics or the
reconstruction of historic sites, but a compositional stance that treats the
inherited archive of spectral, microtonal, extended-technique and electroacoustic
practices as a stratified field to be excavated, re-situated and re-authenticated.
Not a nostalgic return to an imagined origin: a mode of working in which
historically and geographically distant practices are recognised as already folded
into the contemporary horizon, and in which authenticity is no longer tied to origin
alone but to ongoing processes of re-placement and critical contextualisation.

The argument proceeds in five steps. It examines why "culture" has become
conceptually overburdened and analytically unstable for thinking about contemporary
composition. It proposes a shift from culture-blocks to movements, relations and
circuits, introducing **re-authentication** as a more precise term for what is often
vaguely called respect for cultural origins. It distinguishes **vertical** time —
coexisting aesthetic regimes — from **horizontal** time, historical sedimentation.
It describes a practice of **double excavation**: backwards, into the programs
embedded in instruments, techniques and institutions; forwards, into the future
archives that current works will inhabit. Finally it reframes debates about
generative AI from within the ecosystem of contemporary classical composition —
specialist ensembles, festivals, research studios, and experimental tools for sound
analysis, corpus-based composition and live electronics — rather than from the
vantage point of mass platforms.

Throughout, the composer is reimagined less as a solitary originator of form and
more as a **temporal operator**: someone who navigates and reconfigures existing
sound strata, gestures and techniques under conditions of archival saturation and
algorithmic mediation.
